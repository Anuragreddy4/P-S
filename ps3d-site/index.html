Unit 1

Probability is counting inside a sample space

Every probability in this unit is a fraction: favourable outcomes over all outcomes. Conditional probability shrinks the sample space. Bayes' theorem works backwards from an effect to its cause.

Formulas to remember
P(E) = n(E)/n(S)
P(A∪B) = P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B)
P(B|A) = P(A∩B)/P(A)
P(Ek|D) = P(Ek)P(D|Ek) / ΣP(Ei)P(D|Ei)

1.2Bayes' theorem as blocks of output

Unit 2

Distributions are shapes made of probability

A discrete distribution is a set of bars that add to 1. A continuous one is a curve with area 1. The Galton board shows how many small coin flips build the normal curve.

Shape

Binomial → Poisson

Mean vs variance

Binomial: variance npq is less than the mean np. Poisson: variance equals the mean.

2.2Galton board: Binomial becomes Normal

Each ball hits 12 pegs. At each peg it goes right with probability p. Its final bin is the number of rights, which is a Binomial(12, p) count. Drop many balls and the bins take the shape of the normal curve (orange line).

drag to rotate

One peg = one trial

Right is "success". 12 pegs means n = 12 independent trials with the same p.

Central limit idea

A sum of many small random steps is approximately normal. Mean np, s.d. √(npq).

Try it

Set p = 0.2. The pile shifts left (mean = 2.4) and becomes skewed.

Unit 3

A test asks: is Z inside the red region?

Every large-sample test computes Z = (statistic − parameter) / S.E. If Z lands in the critical region, we reject H₀. The confidence-interval scene shows what "95% confident" really means.

3.1The Z-test machine

Choose the test and enter the numbers. The pin shows your Z. The red regions are the critical region for the chosen α and tail. The panel writes the six exam steps for you.

drag to rotate

Acceptance region

The blue middle part. If Z is here, the difference is due to sampling chance. Accept H₀.

Critical region

The red tail area equals α. If Z is here, the difference is too large to be chance. Reject H₀.

Read H₁ from words

"Superior, better, more" → right-tailed. "Less, at least is doubted" → left-tailed. "Same, differ" → two-tailed.

3.2Type I and Type II errors

The front curve is the distribution of Z when H₀ is true. The back curve is the distribution when H₁ is true. Red is α (reject a true H₀). Grey is β (accept a false H₀).

drag to rotate

Trade-off

Make α smaller and the cut moves right. Then β grows. For a fixed n you cannot make both small.

Bigger sample

Increase n. The curves separate (δ grows with √n), so β falls with the same α.

Names

Type I = producer's risk (good lot rejected). Type II = consumer's risk (bad lot accepted). Power = 1 − β.

3.3What "95% confidence" means

40 samples are drawn from a population with true mean μ = 50 and σ = 10. Each bar is one interval x̄ ± z·σ/√n. The glass wall is μ. Red bars miss μ.

drag to rotate

Meaning

About 95 of every 100 intervals contain μ. One single interval either contains μ or not.

Width

Width = 2z·σ/√n. Higher confidence → wider bars. Larger n → narrower bars.

Max error

Half the width is the maximum error E = z·σ/√n. So n = (zσ/E)².

5.1Error squares you can see

Orange squares belong to the least-squares curve. Turn on "my curve" and move a, b, c. Your blue squares grow, and your S is always larger than the best S.

drag to rotate

Why squares

Positive and negative errors would cancel. Squares are always positive, so S = Σe² measures the total misfit.

How the minimum is found

Set ∂S/∂a = 0 and ∂S/∂b = 0. These give the normal equations shown in the panel.

Class-note check

Load "cars inspected" with a parabola. The best curve is y = 10.822 + 1.717x − 0.065x², not the class-note curve.

5.2Logs straighten a curve

Move the slider from "original" to "log scale". The points and the fitted curve bend into a straight line. That is why we fit Y = A + Bx after taking logs.

axb

Unit 6

Correlation tilts the cloud, regression draws two lines

r measures how tightly the points follow a line. There are two regression lines: y on x and x on y. They cross at the means, and the angle between them closes as |r| → 1.

Formulas to remember
r = ΣXY / √(ΣX²ΣY²)
byx = rσy/σx, bxy = rσx/σy
r² = bxy·byx
ρ = 1 − 6[ΣD² + Σ(m³−m)/12] / n(n²−1)

6.1The correlation hill

The hill is the joint density of x and y. The dots on the floor are a sample. Blue is the line of y on x. Orange is the line of x on y. Both pass through (x̄, ȳ).

drag to rotate

r = ±1

The hill becomes a thin ridge, the two lines meet, and the angle is 0.

r = 0

The hill is round. The lines are perpendicular (horizontal and vertical).

Sign rule

byx, bxy and r always have the same sign. If bxy·byx > 1, you labelled the lines wrongly.

6.2Spearman's rank correlation as threads

Front row: ranks in x. Back row: ranks in y. A thread joins each student's two ranks. Straight threads (green) mean agreement. Long slanted threads (red) give large D².

drag to rotate

Ties

Equal values share the average rank (e.g. 3.5, 3.5). Stacked spheres show the ties. Add (m³ − m)/12 for each tie group.

Agreement

All threads straight → ΣD² = 0 → ρ = 1. Ranks fully reversed → ρ = −1.

Exam check

ΣD always equals 0. Check this before you square.
